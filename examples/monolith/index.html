02 — Case study

The house on the cliff.

Site
Limestone shelf, 42 m above the Atlantic
Area
310 m² on two floors
Structure
Two walls, one 6.4 m cantilever
Completed
Spring 2024, after 31 months

A — Plan, 1:200

One bar, laid along the contour.

The buildable shelf is eleven metres deep. We put the whole programme on it as a single bar, turned eleven degrees off the cliff edge so the living floor faces 240° — the bearing of the last light on the longest day.

Roof lifted off at +38.10 · Sun at 17:18, 41° above the sea

B — Section A–A

Two slabs and a promise.

The upper floor bears on two concrete walls pinned nine metres into the rock. The lower slab cantilevers 6.4 metres toward the sea and carries the pool like a tray. Nothing touches the cliff face that doesn't have to.

Cut through the pool and living room · looking north-east

C — Detail, 19:18

Where the water meets the air.

The pool's west wall is a single pane of 19 mm laminated glass, set flush with a 6 mm overflow. At golden hour the water keeps the sun for eleven minutes after it has left the terrace.

Golden hour · sun at 291°, 11° above the sea

03 — Principles

Four rules we haven't broken since 2011.

  1. 01

    Orientation before form.

    We plot the sun over the site for a full year before a single wall is drawn. The plan follows the light — never the other way round.

  2. 02

    One material, honestly.

    Board-marked concrete poured against Portuguese pine. It takes the grain of the wood, the colour of the local aggregate and, over decades, the weather. We don't paint it.

  3. 03

    Shadow is a material.

    A deep overhang does more than any glass coating. We design the shadows first: where they fall at noon in July, how far they reach at four in December.

  4. 04

    Touch the ground lightly.

    Extreme sites are fragile sites. We stand on as few points as possible, rebuild with the rock we excavate, and leave the cliff looking as if we were never there.

04 — Studio

Two studios, 2,750 km of daylight apart.

Lisbon gets 2,800 hours of sun a year. Oslo gets 1,670 — and a June night that never quite gets dark. We work across both, so every house is argued over by people who treat daylight very differently.
